gangline

Many CLI agents. One coordinated team.

install· a team of one· how it works· how it compares· commands· extend· the trail· github

A gangline is the line down the middle of a dog team. Every dog is hitched to it by its own tug line, the lead runs out front, and one look down the line tells the musher who is working — a tight tug is pulling, a slack tug is not.

This is that, for CLI coding agents. Claude Code, Codex, opencode and Pi run as named tmux windows hitched to one session, each used where it is strongest; gang hitches them, sends attributed messages, reads each tug tight or slack, compacts them, and drops them when the work is done. One Bash CLI over tmux — no daemon, message bus, database, or harness plugin, and gang exits after every command.

It is the substrate under a long-running, multi-harness team — not the orchestration on top of it. Who does what, and in what order, is your call, and it lives in a markdown brief you can replace with a directory of your own.

The vocabulary is the command surface, which is why it is worth thirty seconds. You hitch an agent into the line and drop it at a checkpoint; the roster reports a tight tug or a slack tug. That column is not decoration on top of a boolean — mushers watch tuglines more than anything else, because a taut tug is a dog pulling and a bouncing one says something is wrong. gang status performs the same reading on a pane, and the attachment point is the terminal, not code inside the agent.

 tmux: gangline

One message to a Claude Code lead: it hitches a Codex worker, waits for the work, then hitches an opencode reviewer to check it. Real harnesses, real subscriptions, unedited except for cuts through the waiting.

Install

$ curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/adambiggs/gangline/main/install.sh | sh

Needs git, tmux ≥ 2.6 and python3. Clones to ~/.local/share/gangline, links gang into ~/.local/bin, re-run to update. Piping a stranger's script into a shell is your call to make — read it first, or clone and symlink bin/gang yourself.

One thing your harness will not tell you. Gangline launches your harness with your own configuration and sets no permission bypass (one small exception for opencode). An unattended agent cannot answer a dialog and Gangline will not answer one for it, so the setting that stops your harness asking belongs in that harness's own config — the README names the one each wants.

Beyond that, the boundary is the machine. Agents on one host already pass text to one another through files, a shared checkout, or each other's panes; Gangline does not create that path, it makes it routine and gives it structure the ad-hoc versions lack — envelopes a body cannot close from the inside, delivery verified in the pane, and a refusal when the input box is not free. Treat every agent's output as untrusted input to the next, and bound the machine if you need a boundary: a container, a VM, a separate account. A separate $HOME is not one.

A team of one

A team can have one dog in it. gang up with nobody else hitched gives you the context loop by itself: Gangline measures the window, notes each band as you cross it, and near the end compacts you and hands back the thread you asked it to keep — measure, warn, act, instead of you watching a meter and remembering to act on it. 

$ gang up

Solo needs no permissions decision, because you are attached and watching the pane: your harness's interactive defaults are the right ones. The team verbs are all still there and none of them are required — adding a teammate later is gang hitch, and nothing you already set up changes.

How it works

Agents are tmux windows.
Hitching is new-window, dropping is kill-window, watching is capture-pane, and the window name is the agent's identity. Attach and you are looking at your team — the pane is the transcript, and you can type into it yourself at any time.
Every message is attributed, and delivery is verified.
A send is wrapped in a [gang:<sender>#<nonce>] envelope whose nonce is minted from a body that already exists, so the body cannot close its own envelope and open another. It is confirmed by capturing the pane, and only then submitted. No sender means no send. Unverified means a loud failure, never a shrug. This is attribution and not authentication — gangline is single-operator software, and anyone who can type into a pane is already trusted.
Agents manage their own context.
A model cannot feel its own token count, so the substrate measures it and warns at configurable bands. The agent compacts itself at the next clean seam, using a detached resume handoff so work continues unattended.
A new harness costs a profile, not a core integration.
The profile owns launch and model syntax, observable busy and occupied states, compaction, mid-turn input, and optional composer and context readers. Profiles observe the stock TUI: a theme or UI extension that repaints it takes a shadow profile of your own (GANG_PROFILES), re-verified against what you actually run.
Batteries included, every one replaceable.
Agents are hitched with a role brief — lead, worker, reviewer. Point GANG_ROLES at a directory of your own and any brief becomes yours.

Where this sits

gangline is a substrate, not an orchestration framework. The orchestration — who does what, in what order, what happens when one of them stalls — lives in the agent and in a markdown role brief you replace wholesale with GANG_ROLES. gangline owns the layer underneath: how an agent is started, how a message is proven into its input box, what may and may not be claimed about its state, and what becomes of its context over the following six hours.

That boundary is not a positioning statement, it is written into the constitution. Law 9 gives the mission as drive long-horizon multi-agent sessions with minimal machinery, and settles ties with “the answer is prose in an agent's prompt, not code in this repo”. Law 7 forbids gangline from growing any component whose job is watching, policing or coordinating another gangline component — which is what a supervisor loop is. An orchestrator is a thing that decides what runs next; gang never decides that, and is not allowed to.

Which is why the shipped lead / worker / reviewer briefs are deliberately thin. They are a worked example of one way to divide labour, not the product, and swapping them costs a directory. That does not make gangline unopinionated — it is opinionated to a fault, just not about your workflow. It is opinionated about what a tool may claim: that a delivery is not delivered until the box has been seen to change, that an unreadable pane is a third answer rather than a convenient one, that “a UI owns this input box” and “a human must clear it” are different findings and only the first has been established.

The axis is duration, not breadth.
Orchestration competes on breadth — fan out, join, retry, dependency graphs, structured output. Every mechanism gangline actually invented is about surviving time: self-compaction at a clean seam with a detached resume; context bands keyed to how long a context is rather than how full its window is; and a probe that fires each profile's markers at a live harness, because the likeliest thing to break a long-running team is a vendor moving a pixel of chrome next Tuesday. A team that finishes inside one context window needs none of it. A team still working tomorrow morning needs all three, and no amount of fan-out substitutes.

Every tool below solves a real problem, and for several of them the honest answer is “use that one instead” — or “use that one on top of this”, which is not a contradiction. Each is described in the terms it uses for itself.

  • Claude Code agent teams are the orchestration layer gangline deliberately does not ship: a shared task list with dependencies and file-locked claiming, plan approval before a teammate may implement, hooks that fire when a teammate goes idle or a task completes. If every member of your team is Claude Code, start there — it is more integrated than an external process can be. What it is not is cross-vendor, and its coordinator is the lead's own session.
  • claude-squad gives each agent its own git worktree. gangline gives you none. If what you need is four agents on one repo that cannot collide, that is the tool and this is not.
  • amux is a control plane and does far more at that layer: live status and token stats in a browser, a cron scheduler, a kanban board, an iOS app, and a watchdog that compacts context and restarts sessions on its own. gangline warns; your agent is the one that acts.
  • CCB drives a far wider set of harnesses than gangline's four and arranges them into explicit collaboration graphs — A -> B -> C, A,B -> C, A -> B,C — with a background daemon that keeps project state alive after you close the UI, plus a mobile app with remote terminal access and file transfer.
  • SDK frameworks are right when what you want is a program rather than a team: deterministic control flow, retries, structured output, tests. What you give up is the harness — there is no TUI to watch or type into, because there is no harness in the loop.

Where gangline is the wrong tool. No worktree isolation: every agent shares one checkout, and keeping two of them off the same file is yours to arrange. No dashboard, no notifications, no history to browse afterwards — per-agent state lives in tmux window options and dies with the window, which is the deletion path working as designed and also means nothing is kept for you. No auto-restart: an agent that falls over is one you hitch again. Profile version pins go stale every time a harness ships; gang vet tells you, but re-verifying is yours. And it is Bash on tmux, so Windows means WSL. Not on that list: no task list, no dependency graph, no retry policy, no supervisor loop — those are absent on purpose, because they are the layer above.

The client is a terminal. gangline ships no app, no web UI and no mobile client, so there is nothing between you and the agent. SSH or mosh in from a phone, gang attach, and you are in the same tmux session you would be in at your desk: the harness's own interface, every pane, every keystroke. A mobile client gives you a view of what is happening, chosen by whoever wrote it. A terminal gives you what is happening. The cost of that stance is the whole paragraph above — no push notification will ever tell you an agent went idle.

What would make half of this unnecessary. gangline scrapes panes because the harnesses it drives expose no stable programmatic surface to do it any other way, and the design accepts the consequence out loud: text conventions are version-fragile, a TUI update can move a busy marker, and what makes that survivable is that the break is loud and the fix is one line in a profile. The probe exists because of that acceptance, not in spite of it. ACP — which standardises editor-to-agent communication over JSON-RPC much as LSP did for language tooling — is the closest thing to the surface that would make the scraping unnecessary. If the harnesses converge on it, the reading half of this design should be replaced by it, and that would be a good outcome rather than an embarrassing one.

Delivery is measured, not assumed

A send measures the target's input box instead of spraying keystrokes. Gangline reads the box, pastes, and reads again — the box must have changed. Enter goes in as its own keystroke, then Gangline polls until the box differs from the pasted state. An empty composer and a harness's queued message hint are both valid submitted states.

The sender's shell runs before Gangline does. Single-quote literal message prose: backticks or $() inside double quotes execute locally, and Gangline can only verify the already-expanded text it receives.

Matching the text somewhere on screen would verify against an identical earlier send still sitting in the transcript; a changed composer is the harness-independent fact underneath every TUI's rendering. The separate Enter matters too: batch text and newline into one burst and a TUI can read the newline as part of the paste, parking the message unsent. If Gangline cannot verify a submit, it fails loudly; it clears a stranded paste only when it can prove the live composer still contains exactly that paste, and otherwise reports an undelivered paste in status, roster, and patrol.

Context is measured, not felt

Measure, warn, act — the substrate does the first two, the agent does the third. Usage is read through each harness's own introspection: a statusline beacon, a painted readout, or the session file the harness itself writes. Two warning legs run the same band ladder over that one readout — an in-turn hook where the harness has hooks, an ambient cron patrol where it doesn't — and share one band memory, so an agent is warned once per band, not once per leg. Both ends of that ladder are absolute token counts, because context rot tracks how long a context is rather than how full its window is — a 300k context is degraded whether the window is 200k or 1M, so it earns the same warning on both. Every agent's first rung is 120000 and none goes unwarned past 350000: a bigger window is a reason to warn on the same schedule, not a licence to fill it. Between those two the five rungs are spread across the window and drawn closer together toward the top, where the hazard stops being rot and starts being running out of room. 

[gang:patrol] [context-usage] 187k/272k (69%) in context — crossed the
158000-token band. At the next checkpoint (arc boundary), write the handoff to a
file, then run:
gang compact worker --from worker --resume-stdin < resume-file
— it queues behind the turn you are in, and the resume is delivered once
compaction settles.

Where the harness accepts compaction during an active task, the agent then compacts itself at the next clean seam — tests green, a commit made — without being idle first and without anyone watching. Codex 0.145.0 rejects /compact during an active task, so another caller must compact it once it is idle, or Codex must reach its own automatic compaction. The --resume-stdin never rides the input queue directly behind its own compaction, where the turn about to be compacted could swallow it. A detached waiter uses a declared live compaction marker, a large context drop, or a bounded stable-screen fallback before attempting the ordinary verified delivery path. A failure is reported by status and patrol.

Loud refusals, never guesses

gangline's delivery failure mode is a refusal you can read, not a guess you can't. 

$ gang send reviewer --from lead --stdin <<'MESSAGE'
ship it
MESSAGE
gang: 'reviewer' is occupied (authority unknown) — a UI owns its
input box and gang cannot establish who may clear it; a send would act on that
UI instead of landing (gang attach)
  • Gangline sends no keystrokes to occupied agents. A harness whose own UI has taken its input box — a permission prompt, a picker, anything that takes the screen — cannot receive a message, and a send would act on that UI instead, so every delivery path refuses and patrol names the occupancy loudly as what the team is waiting on. What gangline establishes is that the box is taken, never who may free it, so the state qualifies itself (authority unknown) rather than asserting a human is required. Where a profile declares a composer reader, a missing safe composer with no painted busy state reads occupied, never idle.
  • “I cannot tell” is a state, not a default. When pty activity alone has been carrying a busy verdict, it may do so for GANG_ACTIVITY_LIMIT and no longer; past that the agent reads expired (pty activity bound reached). That is neither busy nor idle, and Gangline reports it rather than choosing — a send is refused unless the profile declares a safe composer, because an arm that ran out is a different answer from an agent that was never working, and quietly calling it idle is how one wrong reading becomes permanent.
  • Nudges hold for unsafe panes. Patrol checks Gangline-owned compaction state, a painted busy marker, byte-identical pane captures, and an empty input box before injecting. Holding costs one sweep; interleaving costs the turn.
  • An unread context window is admitted, not hidden. A readout Gangline cannot reach shows as - in the roster and is reported as not patrolled, never quietly skipped.
  • Scrape drift is diagnosed, not endured. Every scraped marker in a shipped harness profile is pinned to live-verified versions; gang vet exits nonzero on drift — and says so when a clean bill can't explain misbehavior, because version pins don't watch your UI mods.

The verbs

commandwhat it does
gang up the whole setup, no arguments: hitches lead in the current repo and puts you in the session
gang hitch <name> -p <profile> -r <role> start an agent — the name becomes its tmux window, its identity, and its handle
gang send <name> --from <you> --stdin attributed, pane-verified message read from stdin; delivered mid-turn where the harness takes input during one
gang roster everyone, with harness, state, and context usage
gang status <name> / context <name> / capture <name> one agent's state, window usage, or screen
gang wait <name> [seconds] block for two consecutive idle readings; parked and expired end it too, so read what it printed
gang compact <name> --from <sender> --resume-stdin submit the harness's own compaction command; a detached waiter attempts the resume and reports delivery failure in status. Native command rejection remains the harness's verdict
gang patrol ambient roster sweep from cron: band warnings in, occupancy and refused inbound out
gang vet [--probe [profile]] check installed versions and file schemas; optionally launch a real turn on a private tmux socket and fire declared markers
gang adopt <name> -p <profile> register an existing team window without launching or briefing it
gang attach / drop / down watch the team live; end one named window; end the whole session
gang profiles / roles list available harness and brief names

Extend it

A profile says how to talk to a harness; a role says what an agent is for. They are the same kind of extension point, and neither is an integration.

  • A profile is sourced shell declaring what Gangline cannot know generically: launch and model syntax, busy and occupied states, compaction, mid-turn input, and optional functions that read context and the input box. Shipped markers nobody has watched live stay unset, and Gangline takes the slower, safer branch rather than guessing.
  • A role is a markdown brief — lead splits work and guards its own context hardest, worker reports what changed and what proves it, reviewer verifies claims rather than reading diffs. The brief is pointed at, not pasted, so it survives compaction.
  • Both are shadowed, not patched. GANG_PROFILES and GANG_ROLES are searched before the shipped directories, so a new harness — or a fix for a TUI that moved under you — costs a file of the same name, never surgery on the installed tree.

The trail

Every term names something real in the system, and the musher's field guide maps all of it — roles, states and verbs, never rules. The ones worth knowing before you start:

on the trailon the team
tight tug / slack tug busy / idle — a pulling dog keeps its tugline taut
hook set occupied — the snow hook is dug in and the team stands anchored, whether the musher set it or it caught on its own
checkpoint the clean seam an arc ends on — tests green, a commit made — where compaction and releases happen
dropped a released window — deliberate and routine; finishing an arc and being released is health, not failure
dog in the basket a finished agent left running instead of released — the anti-pattern dropped exists to prevent
intelligent disobedience a good lead refuses the command onto thin ice — once, short, specific; the musher's word is then final

Of the 1925 serum run's lead dogs, Togo ran the long, dangerous leg while Balto got the statue — credit your working dogs.

One bash script. Profiles and roles are files you can replace. tmux is not a hack under it — tmux is the substrate, and when the metaphor stops carrying weight, every command still reads literally without it.
